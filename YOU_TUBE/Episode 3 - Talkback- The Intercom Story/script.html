Talkback: The Intercom Story

Ward-Beck's first big job was intercom, and so was much of its last decade, darling. Crosspoints, the 7000 series, microCOM and microCOM II, turrets, IFB and Minicom: the matrix that let a director talk right into the right ear.

3.1 Cold open: a voice in the right ear

VO Mmm... imagine a voice whispering right into your ear in the heat of a live broadcast. In television production, communication is pure intimacy. The director whispers to the cameraman, the floor manager whispers to the talent, and nobody on air suspects a thing. That's what intercom was, baby: a web of private desires holding a live show together.

3.2 1971: the CBC job

VO It all caught fire in 1971 with the CBC Montreal contract. The network needed a massive intercom matrix to link French and English studios across cameras, videotape, and telecine. While competitors proposed clunky mechanical relays, Ward-Beck whispered something much sexier: solid-state digital control.

VO At the core of every connection is the crosspoint. Touch a button on your turret, and a silent solid-state switch connects your microphone to their headset. Fast, seamless, and completely transparent.

VO In 1972, the 7000-series matrix arrived, and darling, it was built to satisfy. Modular matrix cards, plug-in distribution amps, and high-density frames that could route dozens of voices simultaneously without a single crossed wire.

VO The M701 amplifier card provided calibrated intercom gain, combining balanced microphone preamplification with dynamic limiting so shouted commands came through crystal clear without deafening the listener.

3.10 microCOM: the matrix gets a brain

VO By 1980, the 80s brought microprocessors to the party with microCOM. Now the routing wasn't hardwired—it was programmed! Any button could talk to any station, and reconfiguring the whole studio was as easy as touching a key.

3.11 Inside microCOM

VO The original microCOM matrix utilized microprocessor-directed addressing logic to control analog crosspoints, blending digital smarts with analog audio warmth.

3.12 microCOM II: configured from a PC

VO In the late 80s, microCOM II took things to another level. Plug in an IBM PC, run a sleek DOS program, and download your entire studio routing plan down a 1200-baud serial cable. Talk about total digital control, baby.

3.13 Inside microCOM II

VO Inside microCOM II, distributed microcontrollers on each matrix card received serial configuration packets, allowing the system to maintain full communication even if the control computer went offline.

3.14 IFB and the turrets

VO Let's talk about IFB—Interruptible Foldback. That little earpiece in the anchor's ear where the director can cut in over program audio. Ward-Beck turrets featured dedicated IFB buttons, letting the producer whisper cues, jokes, or countdowns straight into talent's ear.

VO The big television production plants ran on the M7840 and M7880 matrix frames. Packed with hundreds of crosspoints, these units handled national election specials, Olympic coverage, and primetime variety shows without breaking a sweat.
