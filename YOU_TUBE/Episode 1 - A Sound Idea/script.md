### 1.3 Two men in a garage

**VO:** In April of 1967, Ronald Ward and Rodger Beck commenced formal operations from a garage in Toronto, Ontario. And so it was.

### 1.4 What do you do when it isn't in the catalogue?

**VO:** That was the central question posed by Broadcaster magazine in its 1971 investigative profile. Consider the situation: if you are the CBC, tasked with consolidating French and English network facilities in Montreal and requiring bespoke intercom links across cameras, videotape, and telecine, you turn to Ward-Beck. If you are radio station CKEY in Toronto, moving high above the city into the new Toronto Star building, you call Ward-Beck. Ronald Ward affirmed their strategy plainly: they deliberately pursued 'the complex jobs'—the custom headaches avoided by mass-market competitors. In 1971, Canadian content regulations took effect across the nation's airwaves, prompting Broadcaster magazine to publish a satirical cartoon of a woman clad solely in maple leaves, with the caption: 'Actually, she's an American, but we've managed to maintain Canadian content.' Our viewers may recall the Toronto Maple Leafs have not claimed a championship since 1967, the year Ward-Beck was founded. But Ward-Beck required no symbolic foliage. It was authentic Canadian broadcast engineering, through and through.

### 1.8 Inside the M402A

**VO:** Examining the internal topology of the M402A equalizer, our technical survey reveals a remarkably disciplined circuit architecture. Positioned directly in line with each channel's straight-line fader, it permitted the sound engineer to correct program frequencies prior to summing. The circuit incorporates three active stages: a low-noise transistor preamplifier fed by a shielded input transformer, a unity-gain equalizer stage, and an integrated-circuit output amplifier with actively balanced line drive. Frequency contours are determined by switched resistor-capacitor networks within operational amplifier feedback loops: providing ten decibels of boost at thirty hertz and fifteen kilohertz, five selectable presence centers from seven hundred hertz to five kilohertz, and a field-effect transistor parallel-T notch filter eliminating sixty-hertz power hum by more than thirty decibels. A front-panel pushbutton provided instant comparison, while transient-free switching permitted live adjustment during on-air broadcast.

### 1.9 Inside the M406B

**VO:** Turning to dynamic level control, the M406B compressor-limiter served as the network's automated gain sentry. Operating on a 24-volt direct-current supply, its signal path incorporates a low-noise input amplifier, an actively balanced IC output stage, and a proprietary variable-gain attenuator employing dual field-effect transistors. The sidechain circuit samples the output through a full-wave rectifier, charging timing capacitors to govern attack and release behavior. Front-panel controls provided threshold adjustment from minus twenty to plus twenty dBm, compression ratios selectable at two-to-one, three-to-one, or five-to-one, and attack velocities as swift as one hundred microseconds. An automatic gain makeup circuit ensured consistent program levels without operator intervention.

### 1.11 Inside the M600

**VO:** The workhorse of plant amplification was the M600 universal amplifier card. Designed to fulfill virtually any gain requirement, its circuit topology features a transformer-isolated differential input stage using hand-matched low-noise silicon planar transistors, driving a complementary symmetry push-pull output. The module delivered up to plus twenty-four dBm into a 600-ohm load with total harmonic distortion measured below one-tenth of one percent across the audible spectrum. Internal feedback networks allowed strapping for microphone, line, or bridging gain configurations.

### 1.13 The technology: their own iron, and solid-state switching

**VO:** Two decisive technical decisions established the early authority of Ward-Beck Systems. First: proprietary custom audio transformers. Their engineering nomenclature formed a distinct lineage: 60 for input, 70 for output, and 80 for power. Archival blueprints for the 70-1 output transformer and 80-1 power transformer date to 1966, formally certified by Chief Engineer R. Beck prior to the firm's legal incorporation. The 60-4 input transformer became the vital front end of the M600. Second: solid-state matrix switching. In bidding on the CBC network intercom tender, Ward-Beck proposed solid-state cross-point matrices with digital control logic, dramatically reducing inter-studio cabling between master control and equipment racks. Mr. Beck emphasized that the system could perform automatic self-diagnostic tests upon command. That was 1971; subsequently, the Ampex Corporation engaged Ward-Beck to engineer broadcast-grade versions of that switching topology.

### 1.15 The proposal: CHCH-TV, 9 November 1972

**VO:** To understand the professional posture of Ward-Beck in 1972, one need only review their official contract correspondence. On the ninth of November, President Ronald Ward addressed Mr. W. Jeynes, Director of Engineering at CHCH-TV in Hamilton, Ontario. His letter stated: «Dear Mr. Jeynes. I take pleasure in submitting herewith the Ward-Beck proposal for the supply of an Intercom System and associated control panels and an Audio Control Console designed expressly for sophisticated television production applications.» Note the absence of commercial hyperbole; this was one professional engineer addressing another, resting on established network pedigree: «The equipment proposed comprises well proven design currently in operation throughout the world.» The client list followed: the CBC, the National Film Board, the National Arts Centre, CBS in New York, and WWL-TV in New Orleans. The technical bill of materials was enumerated with precision: «Penny and Giles conductive plastic technology attenuators, Clare-Pendar sealed, illuminated, alternate-action pushbuttons, Weston and Simpson meters, teflon wire, military-grade rotary selector switches, and the extensive use of tantalum and computer grade capacitors.» All specified to «guarantee superior performance in the Hamilton environment.» Those familiar with the industrial atmosphere of Hamilton understand that was a genuine operational specification. Operator ergonomics received equal scrutiny: «Generous knee and leg room exists to maximize operator comfort. In addition, a full-length padded vinyl armrest is provided to minimize operator fatigue.» The fiscal terms were structured with the rigor of a defense contract: forty-nine thousand, two hundred and fifty dollars; delivery in five weeks for the intercom, eighteen for the console; twenty percent upon blueprint approval, seventy upon factory acceptance testing, and ten percent following broadcast installation. Signed: «Yours very truly, R. W. Ward, President.»
