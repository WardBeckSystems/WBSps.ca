Turn the Knob: Filters and Equalizers

Good evening. Tonight, our technical dispatch follows the evolution of frequency shaping at Ward-Beck Systems: from the stepped M402 effects equalizer and M404 hi-lo filter of 1968, through the rigorous A and B redesigns, to the breakthrough 1974 M462 state variable equalizer, the M464 filter, and the M472 channel card—the historic transition where equalizers became fully parametric.

2.1.1 Cold open: something to crow about

VO Spring, 1969. In the pages of Broadcasting and Communications, a bold national advertisement featured a barnyard rooster alongside an imposing audio desk. The headline proclaimed: 'Something to crow about!' The client was Pathé-Humphries of Canada, and the machine was the M1006. The published bill of materials revealed its formidable firepower: eighteen M402 effects equalizers, four M404 hi-lo equalizers, and two M406A film compressors. Eighteen active equalizers housed within a single custom console, delivered by a Canadian manufacturer barely twenty-four months old. Tonight, our special report examines those filters, and the pivotal year of 1974, when Ward-Beck's tone controls ceased clicking and began to continuously turn.

2.1.2 Inside the M404

VO Investigating the circuit architecture of the M404 active filter, our engineering analysis reveals three distinct stages: a low-noise preamplifier stage fed through a shielded input transformer, the filter networks, and an integrated-circuit output amplifier with an actively balanced output. The filter blocks operate at unity gain and can be bypassed completely via front-panel control, converting the module into a flat amplifier. Each active filter section operates in two cascaded stages: an initial resistor-capacitor network providing a 6-decibel-per-octave slope, followed by an isolation amplifier built with dual field-effect transistors where positive feedback adds a further 12 decibels per octave, producing the sharp 18-decibel-per-octave rolloff. Frequency selection is accomplished solely through switched precision resistors. A reverse-polarity protection diode safeguards the internal circuitry against inadvertent 48-volt supply reversal.

2.1.3 1968: the effects equalizer

VO In our inaugural broadcast, we noted the M402A in passing. Tonight, the archival record reveals its origin. Schematic 402-02-010 is dated July 1968, certified by Chief Engineer Rodger Beck. The historical archives also preserve an original manual for the plain M402, with connection diagrams drafted by C. S. O'Connell in August 1968. That specific document carries the official red stamp: 'Property of CFTO-TV Eng. Dept. Do not remove.' Installed upon the console operating face directly in line with the channel's straight-line fader, it was housed behind a black anodized, photo-etched aluminum escutcheon.

2.1.4 Set it up, then punch it in

VO Every operational control on the module was a rotary switch. Three concentric knob assemblies governed low and high boost or cut, presence boost at five calibrated frequencies from 700 hertz to 5 kilohertz, a mid-low shelf, and a 60 hertz notch filter. Ward-Beck guaranteed these switches were completely transient-free. The operating manual explained the studio protocol: with the equalizer switched to OUT, the audio technician configured the equalization curve while the live program remained unaffected. Then, 'at the prescribed time,' the switch was thrown to IN, instantly imposing the prepared acoustic profile onto the broadcast feed. That was the essence of an effects equalizer: an acoustic treatment prepared in advance, then introduced on cue.

2.1.5 The M404 hi-lo pass equalizer

VO Operating in tandem was the companion M404 active hi-lo pass equalizer, introduced in 1968. Identical chassis dimensions, matching panel cutout, interchangeable connector. One rotary selector governed low-frequency cutoff from 70 hertz to 5 kilohertz. A second governed high-frequency cutoff from 500 hertz to 12 kilohertz. Both circuits exhibited a steep attenuation rate of 18 decibels per octave. A third selector engaged high, low, or simultaneous filtering—producing, in the manual's words, a restricted pass-band 'such as telephone effect.' While one filter handled the live transmission, the second could be pre-aligned for the subsequent program cue.

2.1.6 Equalizers by the desk

VO Commercial bulletins marketed these modules as integrated station suites. An advertisement for the M1024 television audio production console emphasized that 'solid-state equalizers, filters, compressors, 20W monitor amplifiers are standard,' declaring the M1024 the 'first choice for the progressive TV studio of the 70's.' Studio wiring blueprints from 1968 confirm the meticulous grounding methodology: each equalizer bank received independent power supplies isolated from program amplifiers, cable shields were grounded at a single point only, and the central system ground bus was securely bonded to the municipal cold-water main.

2.1.7 A and B: the rework

VO In January of 1970, engineering draftsman DJG completed revised interconnect blueprints for the 'M402A and M404A.' These upgraded A revisions introduced a 22-pin gold-plated connector, an illuminated status indicator, and a proprietary solid-state audio switch for channel insertion. Outline diagrams from February 1970 confirm the modules were mechanically profiled for side-by-side array mounting. By 1971, draftsman PHL redrew schematic 402-02-013 for the M402B. The B variant retired the 60 hertz notch and mid-low platform, introducing both presence cut and boost, alongside a dedicated high-pass filter to attenuate low-end ambient studio rumble. Its published brochure listed an American subsidiary: Ward-Beck Systems Inc., Larkin Street, Buffalo, New York.

2.1.8 Steps, not sweeps

VO These early equalization modules relied strictly on stepped detents: five presence selections, ten or eleven discrete cutoff points, calibrated at two decibels per click. Stepped switches ensured absolute repeatability; a control room operator could restore an exact acoustic balance from written log sheets. Yet if an acoustic anomaly occurred at 3 kilohertz and the switch provided only 2.5 or 3.4 kilohertz, the engineer was forced to compromise. In the May 1975 US price list, the M402B and the M404A commanded 415 dollars apiece.

2.1.9 1974: the knobs start to turn

VO In July of 1974, draftsman DM released drawing 4-126 for the equalizer filter board—the circuit heart of the revolutionary M462 equalizer. Moving beyond stepped switches, the front panel presented continuous potentiometers. Each band featured three rotary controls: a continuously variable frequency sweep, a variable Q control adjusting resonance bandwidth from broad to needle-sharp, and a boost-cut control providing 15 decibels in either direction. Dedicated pushbuttons converted high and low frequency bands between peaking and shelving curves. Here was the birth of what modern engineering designates the parametric equalizer: continuous mastery of frequency, bandwidth, and amplitude.

2.1.10 Three levels of equalizer

VO Ward-Beck manufactured this continuous circuit in three distinct tiers. The M462A offered the fundamental configuration: shelving lows and highs with a sweeping mid featuring variable Q, described as 'ideal for use in recording and production audio consoles in all but the most sophisticated systems.' The fully featured M462 provided variable Q and selectable peaking or shelving across all three bands. At the top of the line, the four-band M462B partitioned the midrange into two overlapping sweep sections spanning 100 hertz to 12.8 kilohertz. Its engineering assembly blueprint was completed in September 1974. Official brochures stated the M462B delivered 'sophistication and flexibility not attainable from any other console channel equalizer at the marketplace,' with curves that 'exceed those of most graphic equalizers.'

2.1.11 One filter, many modules

VO The underlying 4-126 circuit board was engineered for widespread deployment across the Ward-Beck line. The identical schematic appears in documentation for the M460 channel input modules and the M463 submaster, demonstrating how channel strips and outboard processors shared a unified acoustic foundation. These outboard units were designed for flexible patchbay routing. Official Ward-Beck specifications allowed stations to order 'patchable audio processing modules, up to a maximum of 12 devices,' in any mix of M462, M462A, M462B, M464, M466 and M467.

2.1.12 Inside the M464

VO Within the chassis of the M464, incoming program audio enters a preamplifier trimmed for unity gain before reaching the high-pass filter stage. An isolation amplifier feeds the initial 6-decibel stage, immediately cascaded into an active 12-decibel section to establish the complete 18-decibel-per-octave attenuation. A heavy-duty rotary switch selects one of eleven precision corner frequencies. The companion low-pass filter is mirrored in design, cascading 6 and 12-decibel stages across eleven cutoff selections from 500 hertz to 15 kilohertz. Dual illuminated pushbutton switches, each with an LED, put either or both filters into the path in any combination. A separate output amplifier card gives an actively balanced output, and transistors in the supply decoupling limit the current if a part fails.

2.1.13 The M464: 18 decibels per octave

VO The filtering circuitry simultaneously received its next-generation platform. The M464 high-pass low-pass filter was certified by schematic in November 1976, with front-panel mechanicals drawn in February 1977. It maintained the steep 18-decibel-per-octave slope of 1968, upgraded to eleven selectable corners per side: from 27 hertz for low cut, and from 500 hertz to 15 kilohertz for high cut. Each filter section now featured an independent pushbutton insert with an illuminated LED. Operational documents outlined their primary broadcast functions: eliminating studio rumble, hum or tape hiss, or deliberately simulating 'limited frequency program paths' such as remote telephone reports.

2.1.14 The M472: a sweep in every channel

VO By 1977, the L-series production consoles demanded an integrated sweep equalizer in every channel position. The M472 blueprint was drafted in June 1977 by MRW, with functional oversight logged by RKB—Chief Engineer Rodger K. Beck. [ARCHIVIST: confirm RKB is Rodger K. Beck.] Configured as a compact plug-in card for the M470 input module insert, it provided a 10-kilohertz high shelf, a peaking midrange sweeping from 400 to 6,400 hertz with a HIGH-LOW Q button, and a low shelf sweeping from 50 to 200 hertz. The technical specification for the L2042 noted each of the twenty inputs 'may be fitted' with an M472, though it could be omitted 'for reasons of economy.'

2.1.15 Where they went

VO Network delivery manifests document the widespread adoption of these 1970s equalizers. Toronto flagship CFTO, on contract 76045, deployed banks of M462, M462A, and M464 units. The L2042 on job 77012 and the L3242 on job 77038 carried M472 modules alongside M462s. The Maryland Center for Public Broadcasting's L3242A, project 790236, incorporated M462, M462B, M464, and M472 cards, with an M464 assigned directly to the video effects switcher. These components remained on active network duty for decades: TVOntario's M1204B in 1988 and multicultural broadcaster CHIN Radio in 1990 both continued to operate with M462A equalizers.

2.1.16 Ward-Beck's word for it

VO Notably, Ward-Beck technical literature rarely used the commercial label 'parametric.' Technical manuals consistently designated them 'state variable equalizer,' after the filter circuit inside, while the LS series catalog termed them 'SVE equalizers.' The term 'parametric' did appear in the 1984 prospectus for the ST2442 and ST3642 consoles: the M462 series was offered as a 'comprehensive four band parametric unit, featuring continuous resolution control of frequency, bandwidth, and amplitude.' By that date, the underlying circuit had served the broadcast industry for a full decade.

2.1.17 Close: after the seventies

VO The state variable topology continued into future generations. In 1984, the M522 became the primary channel equalizer, replacing the continuous Q pot with a dual-setting pushbutton. Archival engineering memos describe the procedure for upgrading legacy M462A units into M522 cards, emphasizing the resulting benefit of adjustable Q control. [ARCHIVIST: who wrote the M462A to M522 conversion note, and when.] Stereo versions followed: the M462C and M562C in 1985, the M472A in 1986, and the M922 in 1994. Yet the fundamental concept of a high-fidelity filter tuned by ear during live broadcast originated with that modest circuit board drawn in July of 1974. And that is the way it was.
