# Episode 7: The WBSps: Preservation Society (2003|36 – 2026|59)

**Runtime target:** 10 min · **Voice-over:** ~1179 words · **Slides:** 88

> In 2003 a twenty-year-old in Saskatoon moved a massive Ward-Beck console out of CBC Regina and started a website to find its manuals. Twenty-three years later that site is WBSps.ca and WBSps.Kuzub.com: a sorted archive of almost 15,000 files. The story of the Ward-Beck Systems Preservation Society, and the passionate creators who pushed the legacy into the future.

## Script and slides

### 7.1 Cold open: it rained on the way to Regina

**VO:** In May of 2003, a historic broadcast console was coming out of Studio 2 at CBC Regina. Installed in September 1982 for 137,000 dollars, this was a masterpiece of analog engineering: twenty M470A preamps, nineteen M462B equalizers, eight M466 compressors, and a 384-point patchbay. The network was decommissioning it. It was headed straight for the landfill. But we couldn't let that happen. Every single module was pulled and packed by hand. The frame was loaded onto Kevin's truck under poly and tarps in the pouring rain, fitting into the elevator with half an inch to spare. True greatness is worth saving.

### 7.2 A kid with a console

**VO:** The kid who bought it was Tony Kuzub—Anthony Peter Kuzub—a twenty-year-old running High Voltage Recording in Saskatoon. [ARCHIVIST: confirm the studio was in Saskatoon; the 2009 letter is addressed there.] When you haul a multi-ton television console home, it doesn't come with an instruction manual. He needed schematics to wire it into his multitrack recorders. So he started searching, asking around, and then he did something radical: he built a website called the Ward-Beck Systems Preservation Society. The mission was dead simple: to keep vintage Ward-Beck equipment running forever by building an open archive of knowledge, documentation, and support.

### 7.3 August 2003: the box from Toronto

**VO:** The turning point came on August 20, 2003. Eugene Johnson at Ward-Beck wrote to say he would dig through the factory archives. Nine days later, an incredible ten-pound box arrived from Toronto. Over sixty original technical manuals: monitor modules, metering, submasters, distribution amps, power supplies, and a prototype POD22 with glowing LED meters. [ARCHIVIST: the archive dates the POD22 to 2014; the 2003 news calls it a prototype.] Then the late-night scanning marathon began, digitizing every single page—including a fifty-page manual for the M466 compressor—so engineers worldwide could access them as PDFs.

### 7.4 The engineer who kept the files

**VO:** Eugene Johnson was the unsung hero who guarded the company's technical heritage. In an era when most corporations threw their paper archives into dumpsters to save warehouse space, Eugene quietly held onto the filing cabinets. Decades of schematics, test specs, and custom console orders from across North America. He understood that these drawings weren't just old corporate files—they were works of art that belonged to human history.

### 7.5 The members

**VO:** Then the community caught fire. From Saskatoon to Vancouver, Los Angeles to London, audio engineers, studio owners, and preservationists joined the society. People who had worked on Ward-Beck desks at CBC, CTV, and NBC began contributing drawings, photos, and personal stories. What started as one kid with a console in Saskatchewan transformed into a global preservation collective.

### 7.6 Dr. Beck

**VO:** At the center of it all stood Chief Engineer Rodger Beck. The society honored him not just as a company founder, but as a visionary circuit designer whose discrete transistor stages and custom transformers gave Canadian broadcasting its signature warmth. His engineering notebooks and checked blueprints remain the sacred texts of our entire archive.

### 7.10 The domain that lapsed

**VO:** In 2011, the original domain at WBSps.com lapsed. A lesser community would have walked away. But our members noticed, bought it back, and kept the flame alive. By 2015, we migrated the entire archive onto modern platforms, and members started sending in their own personal treasures: Len in Saskatoon with the reBECKa console, Dave with the WB24x8x2, Jason with entire racks of M460As. Twenty years of relentless community passion, keeping this incredible hardware alive.

### 7.11 Back at Ward-Beck

**VO:** The relationship with the active Ward-Beck engineering team deepened. Visits to the factory in Scarborough connected vintage preservationists with the engineers building modern openGear and preMO network equipment. Two generations of creators, sharing the same obsessive commitment to audio excellence.
