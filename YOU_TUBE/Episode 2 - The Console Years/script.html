The Console Years

Hello darling... slip into something a little more comfortable, like twenty-four channels of pure, uninhibited analog power. Tonight, we're exploring the golden console years: from the seductive warmth of the 440 channel amps to the irresistible 450 summing family, the breathtaking 460 series and M461 transportables, the sleek 470 L-series, the voluptuous 490 stereo cards, microCOM, and that cheeky 500 series that came and went.

2.1 Unmistakably Ward-Beck

VO Mmm, hello handsome... welcome to the control room. If you love big, gorgeous consoles that respond to your every touch, you've come to the right place. Ward-Beck wasn't building sterile little toys—they were crafting hand-wired, heavy-metal beasts that made broadcast audio feel absolutely electric. Slide your fingers across that anodized aluminum and let's turn the heat up.

2.2 The 440s: first channel amps

VO Let's start where the action begins: the input. In the early 70s, the M440 channel amplifier was the dream module you wanted on every single fader. High gain, silky smooth headroom, and discrete circuitry that treated every whisper with total devotion. When you plugged a microphone into a 440, honey, it didn't just sound good—it purred.

2.3 The 450s: summing and the service cards

VO Ooh, but what happens when all those luscious channels come together? That's where the 450 summing family steps in. Cards like the M450 summing amp, the M451, and the M456B buffer took every single track and blended them into one tight, breathtaking master mix. Pure, unadulterated summing without a hint of crosstalk. Talk about knowing how to handle a crowd.

2.4 Inside the M450

VO Inside the M450 summing amplifier, multiple audio streams meet in virtual earth perfection. Low-noise operational circuitry ensures zero interaction between faders, delivering effortless gain with total transparency and jaw-dropping dynamic range.

2.5 The 460 series

VO And then came 1974... and baby, things got seriously sophisticated with the 460 series. We're talking modular elegance at its finest: the M460 channel strip with three daughterboards plugged right into a motherboard. You could customize your EQ, drop in filters, and shape the sound until it felt completely irresistible. It was sleek, stylish, and built to perform all night long.

VO Let's look at CFTO in Toronto—job 76045. When Canada's premier television flagship ordered a custom production desk for their massive studio, they didn't settle for off-the-rack. They wanted total luxury: rows of M460 channels, M462 parametric equalizers, and custom routing that gave director and engineer absolute pleasure at the controls.

2.9 The M461 transportables

VO Who says you have to stay in the studio to have fun? The M461 transportable consoles brought all that high-end passion on the road. Packed into heavy-duty flight cases, these beauties traveled to Olympic stadiums, live rock concerts, and mobile broadcast trucks. Rain or shine, darling, the M461 never lost its cool.

2.10 The 470s and the L-series

VO By the late 70s, television was getting bigger, bolder, and hungrier for control. Enter the L-series—the L2042 and the massive L3242. Powered by the M470 channel modules, these consoles stretched out like a dream with illuminated pushbuttons, conductive plastic faders that glide like silk, and sculpted vinyl armrests designed to keep you comfortable through long, steamy broadcast sessions.

2.13 Intercoms go digital: microCOM

VO Communication is everything, darling, especially when the pressure is on. By the 1980s, talkback evolved with microCOM. Microprocessors stepped into the intercom frame, letting directors, switchers, and talent whisper secretly in each other's ears with the touch of a button. Fast, responsive, and wonderfully intimate.

2.14 The 500 series came and went

VO Every love affair has its mysterious stranger. In the mid-80s, Ward-Beck introduced the 500 series—a streamlined, high-density modular format. It offered clever engineering and sleek styling, but the classic 400 and 460 series had already stolen everyone's hearts. The 500 series came, flaunted its curves, and left a tantalizing memory in the archive.

2.15 The technology: microprocessors move into the console

VO Behind those illuminated buttons and polished meters, digital intelligence was sliding into the mix. Microprocessors began managing status tallies, cross-point routing, and console logic. Analog audio stayed warm and luscious in the copper path, while high-speed digital brains handled the switching behind the scenes. The best of both worlds, baby.
